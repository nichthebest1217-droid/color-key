A reference for learning musicians

Color Key

Drop a score and see what its harmony is doing. Each chord is colored by its role in the key, and named underneath.

I play music, and when I hear harmony I see color. I made this page so a learner can see what the chords are doing, not only which notes to play. Why this exists

    The language

    The colors

    I wanted a color to answer one question at a glance: is the music at rest, or is it pulling somewhere? So a chord's color comes from what it does in the key, not from which notes it holds. Green is the tonic: home, where a phrase comes to rest. Red is the dominant: the pull back toward home. The same color means the same role in every key.

    Harmony Color

    Nine color families, one for each role a chord can play. Click one to change its color or its name.

      • A lighter shade is a three-note chord. The deeper shade of the same color is a seventh chord.
      • Every note that begins while a chord lasts takes that chord's color, passing notes included.
      • The Roman numeral under the staff names the chord. A letter with a colon, such as c♯: or E:, marks a change of key (lower case for minor).
      • A small question mark means the reading is unsure. Gray means no reading.
      About these colors

      The color says which harmony a note sounds in, not that the note belongs to the chord. In Pitch Color, a note with two names, such as C♯ and D♭, has one color.

      Six of the nine families were agreed with a music-theory teacher, from a score colored by hand. The mediant, the subtonic and the applied chord are my proposals and still wait for review.

      Pitch Color

      Each of the twelve notes has its own color, whatever the key: a rainbow from C up to the next C. It answers a simpler question: which note is this?

        Things to try

        Follow the green. Find every tonic chord, and notice which phrases end on it.

        Watch red turn green. That is a dominant resolving to the tonic. Play the two chords: first the pull, then the rest.

        Disagree with it. If you hear a chord differently, you may be right. Choose your own reading under Make it yours.

          In every score

            What happens to my changes
            • Your changes stay in this browser, on this device. Nothing is sent anywhere. "Save my colors" writes them to a file you can keep or pass on. The score you download carries your colors and the chords you changed.
            • A reading the page remembers applies to every score you open here. It replaces the counts for those notes and is stored in the key the page found. If the key was wrong there, you have taught a reading in the wrong key.
            • What you change is untested. The figures on this page describe the page as published, not your version.
            • "Back to the original" returns the reference colors and names. "Forget everything I taught" removes your readings.

            If you see colors of your own when you hear harmony, this is where to put them.

            The reasoning

            How it reasons

            Every piece is read on its own, from its first bar to its last, in six steps. I chose a method I can explain: each name on the score traces back to chords that experts labeled, and a click shows you the count.

            1. Find the key. A chord has no role until there is a key to have it in. Around each beat, the page asks which key the notes fit best, starting from the key signature. A new key has to hold for a while before it counts, so one borrowed chord does not move it.

            2. Find where chords change. A new chord begins on a beat where a new note enters or the bass moves.

            3. Name each chord from experience. The notes are looked up among 34,803 chords that expert analysts labeled by hand in Mozart's piano sonatas and Beethoven's string quartets. A common reading outweighs a rare one.

            4. Read it in context. Harmony is motion, so a chord is also judged by its neighbors. Which chord tends to follow which was counted in the same analyses, over 39,677 steps from one chord to the next. A whole chain is then chosen together, so each name fits its own notes and its neighbors.

            5. Read it again, by rule. The same notes are read a second time with no counts, by stacking them in thirds as a harmony class would. A note that sounds only briefly counts for less.

            6. Color it. The chord's role gives the color, and the name goes under the staff. The score shows the first reading. Where the second differs, the page says so.

            A second reading

            Steps 3 and 4 read by experience. The page remembers what experts called these notes, the way you know a familiar word without spelling it. But experience can only repeat its examples.

            Step 5 reads by rule, the way harmony is first taught, so it can read a chord nobody has labeled before. Stack the notes in thirds. The bottom of the stack is the root. The root's degree in the key, with the kind of chord, gives the numeral and the color. In C major, G, B, D and F stack on G, the fifth degree: V7, a dominant, deep red.

            The two readings share the key and the places where chords change, but not how a chord gets its name. So where they give the same color, that color is more often the expert's. Where they differ, neither is proved wrong. It is a place to look twice, and to listen.

            They can also agree and both be wrong: a wrong key misleads them together.

            The rules use no counts of what experts wrote. A few of their choices, such as which chords count as the dominant of another chord, were settled while looking at labeled Mozart and Beethoven movements. So the second reading shares that school of analysis.

            Reading a printed page

            A PDF or a picture is read on your device, in the order a person reads.

            1. Find the staves: five thin lines, the same distance apart.
            2. Find the signs on each staff: noteheads, clefs, accidentals, barlines, rests. A small trained network does this. It learned only from pages engraved for this project, never from a scan.
            3. Reason from the signs. The clef names the lines and spaces. The key signature and the accidentals earlier in the bar alter the notes.
            4. Color each note by its pitch, on the page itself.

            Point at a note and the page gives its reason, for example: "G3: a notehead on space 4 (from the bottom), bass clef, 1 flat in the key signature. Natural because of an accidental earlier in the same bar, which still holds."

            It does not read rhythm yet. Harmony Color needs to know which notes sound together and for how long, so a picture gets Pitch Color only.

            The findings

            What I found

            I measured the page against expert analysts. These are the figures I would want before trusting it in a lesson. "Of the music" means by how long the music lasts, not by the number of chords.

            66.9%

            of the music took the color family an expert gave it

            About two-thirds of the music matches an expert.

            Counted on 163 pieces that had no part in building the page, each chord read from its own notes (steps 1 to 3). The page found the expert's key for 74.6% of the music. Coloring every chord as the tonic would match for 38.0%. I fixed this test in advance and ran it once.

            How it was counted

            For about a third of the music, you will see a different color family from the expert's. The test compared the color family, not the full numeral or the shade, and I have not rerun it with steps 4 and 5.

            The pieces: 135 songs, 24 keyboard pieces (23 preludes from Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier and one Chopin étude) and 4 pieces over a repeating bass. Songs matched for 69.9%, the keyboard pieces for 52.6%. Each piece was compared with one expert's analysis, and 108 of the 163 are credited to the same analyst.

            The three examples on this page are pieces I had in front of me while building it, so they flatter it: it matches an expert on 88.5% of the Chopin, 72.9% of the Bach and 81.2% of the Mozart.

            70.3%→72.9%

            agreement on the color family, before and after step 4

            Context adds a little.

            Counted on the 24 Mozart and Beethoven movements I used to set it. Reading a chord with its neighbors helps a little, and not everywhere. It pulls a reading toward the usual progressions, which helps ordinary passages and can cost unusual ones.

            How it was counted

            The setting was chosen on these same movements, which come from 6 compositions. In 4 of the movements agreement fell. On 25 further movements, which earlier work had already examined, it rose from 68.3% to 68.9%. Both pairs of figures were measured with the counts of 75 other movements, not with the full counts the page uses.

            In the first movement of Beethoven's "Moonlight" Sonata, where the page hears the opening in the wrong key, agreement with a hand analysis fell from 68.1% to 64.5%.

            93.4%

            of the notes on scans it had never seen were read exactly right: letter name, sharp or flat, and octave

            A printed page is read mostly right.

            Good enough to color a page, not good enough to trust every note. Only 529 of the 1,493 scanned lines of music came out with every note right, about one line in three. Check the colors against the print.

            How it was counted

            Counted on 86,546 notes of scanned piano music. A line here is one system: both hands across the page. None of it was used to teach the reader, and I read the result once, after the reader was fixed. Of the notes it colored, 92.5% were right, so a colored page also holds some notes that are wrong or not there.

            On pages printed for the test (8 movements, each in four music fonts) it read 99.3% of the notes in the one test font it had also learned from (it learned from five), 99.0% and 98.2% in two it had never seen, and 74.1% in a third unseen font, one that imitates handwriting, where it found the treble clef in 17 of 771 places. One of those movements had already been used while I developed the reader. Its errors on scans are notes it adds or misses, key signatures undercounted on crowded old engravings, octave signs, which it does not read, and notes far above or below the staff.

            68.9%

            of the music was given the same color family by both readings

            When the two readings agree, the color is more often the expert's.

            Counted on 24 Mozart and Beethoven movements, the ones I used to set step 4. Where the two readings agreed, their color was also the expert's 81.2% of the time. Where they differed, the color on the page was the expert's 54.0% of the time. When both give the same color, you can lean on it more. When they differ, look and listen for yourself.

            Both readings use the same key, the same chord boundaries and the same note spelling. If the key is wrong, both are wrong together.

            How it was counted

            The readings differed for 15.7% of the music, and there the rule's color was the expert's 32.7% of the time. For the rest (15.3% of the music) the two were not compared: the rules gave no chord, the lowest note was not part of the rule's chord, or the first reading gave none. There the color on the page was the expert's 55.1% of the time.

            On 25 further movements, which earlier work had already examined, the readings agreed for 67.2% of the music; the color was the expert's 79.1% of the time where they agreed and 49.9% where they differed.

            The two readings share the key, so none of this says anything about wrong keys. Of the music where they agreed, 14.8% was read in a key the expert did not write. These are Mozart and Beethoven movements, the composers the first reading learned from, and its settings were chosen on these same movements. Other music will give other figures.

            One more: it says when it is unsure

            On the same 163 tonal pieces, 17.5% of the music carried a question mark or stayed gray. On Messiaen's eight Préludes, which rest on his modes more than on tonic and dominant, it was 57.1% to 90.5% of each piece. Many question marks are the page telling you the music lies outside what it knows. The marks are not complete: some chords there still pass without one. Measured with steps 1 to 3. The Préludes were read by machine from a scan of the printed score, and some wrong notes remain.

            None of these figures says that color helps anyone learn. I have not tested that.

            How far to trust it

            Keep in mind

            Before you rely on a color, know where the page is weak.

            It is a reference, not an answer key.
            Harmony can be heard in more than one way, and analysts disagree with each other. Expect about two-thirds of the music to match an expert.
            It knows the Classical style best.
            Its examples are Mozart and Beethoven. In later or freer music, many question marks are the page telling you the music lies outside what it knows.
            A question mark is a hint, not a guarantee.
            Unmarked readings can be wrong too.
            A wrong key spoils what follows.
            The colors after it are wrong with it. Both readings count from the same key, so they can agree and still be wrong.
            Printed pages are read less surely than files.
            The reader does not read rhythm or octave signs, and it can miscount a key signature on a crowded old engraving. I have not tested photos taken with a camera.
            The colors are a choice.
            For some kinds of color vision the green and the red are hard to tell apart. The Roman numeral is always there as a second cue, and the colors can be changed.
            Whether colors help someone learn has not been tested.
            That is the hope behind the project, not a result.

            From me

            Why this exists

            Sheet music is black and white. For many learners, reading it is the first wall.

            I play music, and someone close to me is neurodivergent and plays too. During a summer internship I was asked to build something useful for autistic children. A music therapist showed me how strongly visual cues and music connect. Playing an instrument can be good practice for fine motor skills, yet the notation is often what stands in the way.

            My first idea was a rainbow: one color for each pitch, from C up to the next C. It is still here, as Pitch Color. But a rainbow only says which note to play. I wanted the page to show what the harmony is doing: where it rests, where it pulls, where it turns away. That became Harmony Color.

            I also have sound-to-color synesthesia. When I hear harmony, I see color. The composer Olivier Messiaen wrote about the colors of his own chords. People do not see the same colors. So this page keeps two things apart. The name of a chord is something I can check against experts. Its color is a choice, and you are free to change it.
