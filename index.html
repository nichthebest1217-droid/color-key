Color Key

Drop a score and see its harmony in color. Each chord is colored by what it does in its key, and named underneath.

Or try one:

    The colors

    A chord's color comes from what it does in the key at that moment. The tonic is green and the dominant is red in every key, so the same color always means the same role.

    Harmony Color

      • A lighter shade is a three-note chord. A deeper shade of the same color is a seventh chord.
      • Every note that begins while a chord lasts takes that chord's color, passing notes included.
      • The Roman numeral under the staff names the chord. A letter with a colon, such as c♯: or E:, marks a change of key (lower case for minor).
      • A small question mark means the reading is unsure. Gray means no reading.

      Pitch Color

      In Pitch Color, each of the twelve notes has its own color, whatever the key.

        How a score is read

        Every piece is read on its own, from its first bar to its last. Nothing is sent anywhere: the reading runs in your browser.

        1. Find the key. Around each beat, it asks which key the notes fit best. It starts from the key signature, and a new key has to hold for a while before it counts, so one borrowed chord does not move the key.

        2. Find where chords change. A new chord begins on a beat where a new note enters or the bass moves.

        3. Name each chord from its notes. The notes are looked up among 34,803 chords that expert analysts labeled by hand in Mozart's piano sonatas and Beethoven's string quartets. Each candidate name keeps its count.

        4. Read it in context. Which chord tends to follow which was counted in the same analyses, over 39,677 steps from one chord to the next. The whole chain of chords in a key is then chosen together, so each name fits both its own notes and the chord before and after it. A chord that simply continues stays the same chord.

        5. Color it. The chord's role gives the color, and the name is written under the staff.

        Click any Roman numeral in the score to see why that chord was read that way.

        Keep in mind

        • It is a reference, not an answer key. Harmony can be heard in more than one way, and analysts disagree with each other.
        • It knows the Classical style best. Its examples are Mozart and Beethoven. In later or freer music you will see many question marks. That is the page telling you the music lies outside what it knows.
        • How often it agrees with an expert. On 163 pieces it had never seen, the reading without step 4 chose the expert's key for 74.6% of the music and the expert's color for 66.9%. Adding step 4 raised the color figure from 70.3% to 72.9% on a separate set of 24 Mozart and Beethoven movements. Expect about two chords in three to match.
        • The key is the hard part. When the key is wrong, the colors after it are wrong with it.
        • The colors are a choice. For some kinds of color vision the green and the red are hard to tell apart. The Roman numeral is always there as a second cue.
        • Whether colors help someone learn has not been tested. That is the hope behind the project, not a result.

        Why this exists

        Sheet music is black and white. For many learners, reading it is the first wall.

        I play music, and someone close to me is neurodivergent and plays too. During a summer internship I was asked to build something useful for autistic children. A music therapist showed me how strongly visual cues and music connect. Playing an instrument is good practice for fine motor skills, yet the notation is often what stands in the way.

        My first idea was simple: color every note by its pitch, a rainbow from C up to the next C. That view is still here, under Pitch Color.

        Then I wanted the colors to say more. Not which note this is, but what the harmony is doing. That is Harmony Color. It is meant as a reference for learning musicians: a way to see how sounds relate, and how one moves to the next.

        I also have sound-to-color synesthesia. When I hear harmony, I see color. The composer Olivier Messiaen wrote about the colors he saw in his own chords and modes. People do not see the same colors, and no palette is the correct one. So this page has two layers. The first names the harmony. The second gives it a color, and that second layer is free to change.
